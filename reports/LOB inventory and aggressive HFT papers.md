# Five milliseconds cannot buy order-flow alpha

The literature on limit order books is unusually clear about where its own results stop being useful, and the boundary sits almost exactly at our design point. Contemporaneous explanatory power is enormous and well replicated: order flow imbalance explains **about 65% of 10-second mid-price variance** across 50 US stocks ([Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)), and queue imbalance improves next-move classification by **50–60% over a null model** in large-tick stocks ([Gould & Bonart](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03492)). Forward-looking power at any horizon a strategy can trade is close to zero: the best recent study reports a **negative out-of-sample R² of −0.37** for the same variable one minute ahead ([Cont, Cucuringu & Zhang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.13213)). Three things transfer intact: the hard-constraint quoting architecture of Guéant–Lehalle–Fernandez-Tapia, the identity that values a queue position as fill probability times (premium minus adverse selection), and a validation standard brutal enough to reject most published results. Four do not: latency races decided in microseconds, queue rent at real-world queue lengths, impact-decay PnL, and deep learning. Our simulator is both a worse and a better laboratory than the papers' markets. Worse, because our orders never move the hidden price, so resiliency and manipulation strategies have no causal channel. Better, because the counterfactual is observable, which lets us measure fill hazard, queue value and markout directly rather than inferring them from a hundred million messages.

## The book is legible at event scale, and almost nowhere else

Order flow imbalance has a clean, near-mechanical relation to price, and its definition matters because it refuses to distinguish causes. For each event at the touch with bid/ask prices $P^b, P^a$ and queue sizes $q^b, q^a$:

$$e_n = \mathbf{1}\{P^b_n \ge P^b_{n-1}\}q^b_n - \mathbf{1}\{P^b_n \le P^b_{n-1}\}q^b_{n-1} - \mathbf{1}\{P^a_n \le P^a_{n-1}\}q^a_n + \mathbf{1}\{P^a_n \ge P^a_{n-1}\}q^a_{n-1}$$

with $\mathrm{OFI}_k$ the sum over the interval and $\Delta P_k = \alpha_i + \beta_i\,\mathrm{OFI}_k + \varepsilon_k$ fitted on 10-second mid changes in ticks. The construction treats a market sell and a cancel of a resting buy as equivalent "since they have the same effect on the size of the bid queue" ([arXiv:1011.6402](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)). That equivalence is what makes the relation linear: on 50 S&P 500 stocks in 2010, across 273 half-hour subsamples per stock, **mean R² = 65%**, per-stock means 47–79%, significant at 95% in about 98% of samples, and a quadratic term raises R² only to 68%. The slope is inversely proportional to depth, $\beta = c/D^{\lambda}$ with $\lambda \approx 1$, so one lot of imbalance moves the expected mid by roughly $1/(2D)$ ticks. With $D \approx 900$ lots at our touch, moving the mid one tick takes about **1,800 lots of net touch imbalance**, twice the visible queue and eighteen times our maximum inventory. That single number explains why the book feels deep: the mechanical price response to anything we can do is sub-tick.

Multi-level aggregation improves the fit and compresses the book to one factor. Adding levels 2–5 cuts out-of-sample RMSE by **65–75% for large-tick stocks** and 15–30% for small-tick ones, with Ridge rather than OLS because level flows are collinear ([Xu, Gould & Howison](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06230)). PCA-aggregated "integrated OFI" lifts contemporaneous out-of-sample R² from **64.6% to 83.8%**, and the first principal component explains **over 89% of the variance** of the level-wise flow vector ([Cont, Cucuringu & Zhang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.13213)), which licences an $O(1)$ implementation: after an offline fit, one loading vector stands in for the whole depth profile.

Queue imbalance is the cheapest directional statistic and the one that maps most directly onto a one-tick book. With $I = Q_b/(Q_b+Q_a)$, weighted mid $W = IP^a + (1-I)P^b$, and microprice $P^{\text{micro}}_t = \lim_{i\to\infty}E[M_{\tau_i}\mid\mathcal{F}_t]$ over successive mid-change times, the microprice is a martingale by construction and equals the mid plus a spread-and-imbalance correction ([Stoikov, DOI:10.1080/14697688.2018.1489139](https://doi.org/10.1080/14697688.2018.1489139)). The expansion converges fast enough to truncate at $\tau_6$ and is evaluable in $O(1)$ from a small pre-calibrated Markov chain. The actionable numbers are Gould and Bonart's: logistic regression of the next mid move on $I$ across 10 liquid Nasdaq stocks rejects the null for all 10, with binary-classification improvement of **50–60% for large-tick names** and 10–30% for small-tick names, falling to 20–30% and 2–6% when the prediction must be probabilistic ([arXiv:1512.03492](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03492)). Where the spread is one tick, $I$ is a complete description of the touch and the microprice's extra content, the spread-dependent correction, collapses. Weighted mid is no substitute: it moves on every imbalance update, is not a martingale, and is "quite noisy for the purpose of high frequency volatility estimation" ([Stoikov, SSRN 2970694](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2970694)).

Volatility needs a clock, not an event counter. Realized variance on every tick diverges linearly in the number of observations, $[Y,Y]^{(all)}_T \to 2nE[\varepsilon^2]$, and scaled by $(2n)^{-1}$ it estimates the variance of microstructure noise rather than integrated variance ([Zhang, Mykland & Aït-Sahalia, JASA 2005](https://www.stat.uchicago.edu/~mykland/paperlinks/Twoscales-090404.pdf)). Noise is negligible for sampling coarser than about **20 seconds** ([Bandi & Russell, JFE 2006](https://faculty.chicagobooth.edu/-/media/faculty/jeffrey-russell/research/separate.pdf)), whose fitted optima are **4.5 minutes** for SBC and **7.5 minutes** for XOM. A one-second mid sample is the right compromise for us: fine enough for regime detection, coarse enough that the bid-ask bounce does not dominate.

Order lifetime is the last measurable object, and the real-market numbers are too extreme to import. Market-wide, **60% of limit orders are cancelled within 10 seconds and 98.4% within 10 minutes**; on Island in 2001–02 about 25% died within 2 seconds and 40% within 10, with fleeting orders making up 27.7% of visible orders ([Hasbrouck & Saar, JFM 12(2)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/jhasbrou/Research/Working%20Papers/HS6.pdf)), and in a 2010 Nasdaq sample **30% of cancellations fall in the shortest 5 ms bin** ([Ye et al., SEC seminar paper](https://www.sec.gov/divisions/riskfin/seminar/ye031513.pdf)). Market-wide fill rates for posted orders on the NYSE in 2010–11 were **below 3%** ([Review of Finance 30(2), 2025](https://academic.oup.com/rof/article/30/2/459/8277182)). Our tape has none of this: cancels fire on independent exponential timers, so displayed depth is a queue with a known decay rate rather than a population of strategic intentions.

## Forecasting dies in the gap between one event and one minute

The negative results are the most valuable part of this literature, because each one closes a branch that looks attractive on a chart.

OFI forecasts nothing once temporal ordering is enforced. Cont, Cucuringu and Zhang fit on one window and test on the next and report one-minute-ahead out-of-sample R² of **−0.37** own-price and **−0.10** with cross-impact terms, where negative means worse than the unconditional mean; they are explicit that earlier work reported in-sample R² or cross-validated in a way that "damaged the chronological order of the time-series data" ([arXiv:2112.13213](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.13213)). The pattern across the whole survey: contemporaneous power is huge, predictive power is approximately zero, and the residual lives at the event scale inside ten to twenty seconds.

Trade imbalance is the wrong regressor, and order splitting is why. Once OFI is in the regression the trade-imbalance coefficient "must be indistinguishable from zero", and the price-volume relation is "noisy and less robust" ([arXiv:1011.6402](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)). Market-order signs are long-memory with $C(\ell)\sim\ell^{-\gamma}$, $\gamma\approx 0.5$, precisely because metaorders are split, so a naive signed-volume signal measures one parent order repeatedly ([Bouchaud, arXiv:0903.2428](https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.2428)). What restores short-range predictability is the mixture with limit orders and cancels: returns stay autocorrelated for about **100 events, roughly 10–20 seconds**, beyond which memory is largely cancelled ([Eisler, Bouchaud & Kockelkoren](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.0900)). On a Poisson tape there is no splitting at all, so every "three buys in a row" signal is noise by construction.

Impact is not permanent, so "follow the big trade" is not a strategy. Aggregate impact is concave, close to $\sqrt{Q/V}$ with a prefactor of order unity ([Toth et al., Phys. Rev. X 1, 021006](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.1694)); direct estimation on about 700,000 institutional orders gives permanent impact $I = \gamma\sigma(X/V)(\Theta/V)^{1/4}$ with **γ = 0.314 ± 0.041** and temporary impact $J = I/2 + \eta\sigma|X/(VT)|^{3/5}$ with **η = 0.142 ± 0.0062**, explicitly rejecting a pure square root ([Almgren, Thum, Hauptmann & Li, Risk 18(7)](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/costestim.pdf)). With sign autocorrelation $\gamma\approx 0.5$, efficiency requires the propagator to decay with $\beta = (1-\gamma)/2 \approx 0.25$, a fat-tailed decay where median reversion is fast and mean reversion slow ([arXiv:0903.2428](https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.2428)). That fat tail is the mechanism behind adverse selection on resting orders, and why a markout-based fill filter beats a wider spread.

Tick-level returns are negatively autocorrelated, which is a gift to makers and a tax on chasers. Roll's model implies $\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta p_t,\Delta p_{t-1}) = -(s/2)^2$, so where the spread dominates the innovation the first-order autocorrelation is strongly negative ([Roll, J. Finance 39(4)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1984.tb03897.x)), and variance ratios below one at short horizons are the canonical Lo–MacKinlay finding ([RFS 1(1) 41–66](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/1.1.41)). A symmetric maker filled on both sides earns the bounce; a taker chasing a one-tick move pays it.

Deep learning's own numbers decay exactly as this account predicts. DeepLOB reports 3-class accuracy of **78.91% and F1 77.66 at k=10** on FI-2010, but on the authors' own LSE data, trained on five stocks and tested out of sample, accuracy falls monotonically to **70.17% at k=20, 63.93% at k=50 and 61.52% at k=100** ([Zhang, Zohren & Roberts, IEEE TSP 67(11)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). Two facts strip the headline: the celebrated k=10 improvement over the previous attention-bilinear model was **F1 77.66 versus 77.63**, and the label is the sign of a forward moving average against a **0.2% threshold**, which the authors themselves call "essentially detrimental for designing trading algorithms". Re-run on real Nasdaq data with **tick-level labels**, F1 lands at 0.45–0.7 before thresholding and the profit-relevant statistic, the probability of completing a correct round trip from the chronological signal sequence, is **0.06–0.15**; above a 0.5 probability threshold it collapses toward zero for small- and medium-tick stocks, so filtering for confidence destroys the ability to trade ([Briola, Bartolucci & Aste, Quant. Finance 25(7)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12315853/)).

VPIN failed its own test. The claim was that a real-time toxicity meter reaches extreme values before turmoil. The measurement is that it "did not reach an all-time high prior, but rather after, the flash crash", is "a poor predictor of short run volatility", and its content is "due primarily to a mechanical relation with the underlying trading intensity" ([Andersen & Bondarenko, JFM 17:1–46](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2013.05.005)). Controlling for volume and volatility there is "no evidence of incremental predictive power", and VPIN built with perfect trade classification gives results diametrically opposite to the bulk-volume version, so the classification step was doing the work ([JFM 17:53–64](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1386418113000475)). The practical conclusion holds after the authors' rejoinder ([Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara, JFM 17:47–52](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1386418113000293)): a level-based toxicity threshold fires in every busy period and never in a quiet crash. The generalisable warning is that almost every "predictive microstructure metric" is a monotone transform of volume times volatility, so any signal must be tested as an increment over that baseline. The survey literature reaches the same conclusion from another angle: "many such models poorly resemble real LOBs and several well-established empirical facts have yet to be reproduced satisfactorily" ([Gould et al., arXiv:1012.0349](https://arxiv.org/abs/1012.0349)). A fitted book model is a description, not a generator of alpha.

## Quoting theory survives as an architecture, not as a formula

Avellaneda–Stoikov reduce market making to two separable decisions. Fills are Poisson with $\lambda^b(\delta^b) = Ae^{-k\delta^b}$ and $\lambda^a(\delta^a) = Ae^{-k\delta^a}$, and the agent maximises $E[-\exp(-\gamma(X_T + q_TS_T))]$ ([A&S, Quantitative Finance 8(3):217–224](https://doi.org/10.1080/14697680701381228)). The closed form is

$$\delta^{b*} \approx \tfrac{1}{\gamma}\ln\!\left(1+\tfrac{\gamma}{k}\right) + \tfrac{1+2q}{2}\gamma\sigma^2(T-t), \qquad \delta^{a*} \approx \tfrac{1}{\gamma}\ln\!\left(1+\tfrac{\gamma}{k}\right) + \tfrac{1-2q}{2}\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)$$

which add and subtract to a reservation price $r = s - q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)$ and a spread $\psi = \tfrac{2}{\gamma}\ln(1+\gamma/k) + \gamma\sigma^2(T-t)$ **independent of inventory**. The empirical pattern is therefore pure mid skew at constant width, exactly what Hendershott and Menkveld find in twelve years of NYSE specialist data: the intermediary "lowers the midquote on long positions and raises it on short positions", while "relative to midquote changes the bid-ask spread appears constant across states" ([JFE 114(3):405–423](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2014.08.001)). But the formula is a terminal-liquidation rule posing as a quoting rule. Guéant, Lehalle and Fernandez-Tapia show it is "nothing but the Taylor expansion of the optimal quotes for $t$ close to $T$", that the true optimum is "almost independent of $t$ as soon as $t$ is far from the terminal time", and that "in the absence of inventory limits, no proof of optimality is available ... and their admissibility appears to be an open problem" ([arXiv:1105.3115](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.3115)). With a literal $T-t$ the skew collapses as the session ends, the opposite of what a penalty model wants.

The ergodic solution is the one to implement, and it is where the hard constraint lives. GLFT solve the problem exactly by a change of variables that makes the HJB equation a tridiagonal linear ODE, with the bound entering as two boundary terms:

$$g = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2\gamma}{2kA}\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)^{1+k/\gamma}}, \qquad h = \frac{1}{\gamma}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\right), \qquad r = s - qg, \qquad \text{quotes} = r \mp (h + g/2)$$

with $\psi = 2h + g$. The bound acts simply: at $q = Q$ the bid term is dropped from the HJB equation and at $q = -Q$ the ask term is dropped, because "a market maker with inventory $Q$ ... will never set a bid quote" ([arXiv:1105.3115](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.3115)); in Guéant's framework the intensities carry indicators $\lambda^b = \Lambda^b(\delta^b)\mathbf{1}_{q<Q}$ and $\lambda^a = \Lambda^a(\delta^a)\mathbf{1}_{q>-Q}$ ([arXiv:1605.01862](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.01862)). That is a guarantee rather than a trade-off, and with a $10{,}000 per second step penalty it is the only defensible architecture: shut the increasing side, do not price the breach.

The numbers are instructive and, for a one-tick book, sobering. With GLFT's reference calibration $\sigma = 0.3$ tick·s$^{-1/2}$, $A = 0.9$ s$^{-1}$, $k = 0.3$ tick$^{-1}$, $\gamma = 0.01$ tick$^{-1}$, one gets $g \approx 0.068$ ticks per lot, $h \approx 3.28$ ticks and $\psi \approx 6.63$ ticks; raising risk aversion tenfold to $\gamma = 0.1$ narrows the spread to 5.98 ticks while tripling the slope, a clean demonstration that "more risk aversion" does not mean "wider quotes" here ([arXiv:1105.3115](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.3115)). A spread floor of 3.3 half-ticks cannot exist where the grid pins the spread at one tick, so $h$ is **censored by the tick** and cannot be a free parameter; control must act through skew, side selection and size instead. The portable quantity is the ratio $gQ/\psi$, the concession in spreads the model will give to shed a full cap. With GLFT's numbers it is $0.068\times100/6.63 \approx 1.0$ spread, which is sensible; with $k = 1$ tick$^{-1}$ and $A = 1$ s$^{-1}$, describing fills that die within a tick, $g \approx 0.035$ and the ratio is about 3.5 spreads, at which point the "maker" is crossing the book at the worst moment. A ratio far above one means clipping the skew at roughly a tick and letting side-shutoff and size do the rest.

Risk aversion is one parameter, not two. Guéant writes the Cartea–Jaimungal–Penalva criterion as $E[X_T + q_TS_T - \ell(|q_T|) - \tfrac12\gamma\sigma^2\int q_t^2dt]$ and shows both objectives satisfy the same ODE family with $\xi = \gamma$ or $\xi = 0$, so **a running penalty $\phi q^2$ with $\phi = \tfrac12\gamma\sigma^2$ is exactly CARA risk aversion $\gamma$** ([arXiv:1605.01862](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.01862)). Code exposing both $\gamma$ and $\phi$ double-counts risk. The same family gives the alpha rule: with drift $\mu$ the ergodic skew becomes $q_{\text{eff}} = q - \mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)$, so at $\gamma = 0.01$, $\sigma = 0.3$, a drift of 0.01 ticks per second is worth about **11 lots of inventory** ([arXiv:1105.3115](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.3115)). That is the right order of magnitude for asking whether a signal deserves action, and most signals we can measure are worth fewer lots than the noise in our inventory target.

Cartea–Jaimungal–Penalva supply the two features the ergodic model lacks: a running penalty with a term proportional to $\phi(1\mp2q)(T-t)$, and a terminal penalty whose effect is that "as the dynamic trading strategy approaches the terminal date, orders are posted nearer to the midquote to induce mean-reversion to zero in inventories" ([CJ&P, SIAM Review 60(4), DOI:10.1137/18M1176968](https://doi.org/10.1137/18M1176968)). Their alpha result is a **spread** skew of $\pm E\int_t^T\alpha_u du$, not a mid shift: a positive signal widens the sell half-spread to avoid being picked off and narrows the buy half-spread to participate. Under 5 ms latency that asymmetry is safer than GLFT's mid shift. Our session has no terminal date, so the $(T-t)$ structure must become a constant effective horizon, or the penalty term vanishes and the policy degenerates to a symmetric maker.

Robust control gives three directional results that hold across formulations: ambiguity about drift or arrival rates makes the maker mean-revert inventory **faster** and increase **total** depth, while ambiguity about the execution price makes it quote **tighter** ([Cartea et al., SIAM J. Financial Mathematics](https://doi.org/10.1137/16M106282X)). Our dominant uncertainty is the tape's generator, whose parameters change between rounds, so the drift-and-arrival branch applies: bias toward faster mean reversion and more depth, not tighter quotes. Formally the ambiguity-averse maker behaves as if the mid drifts at $\eta^*(q) = \alpha - \phi_\alpha\sigma^2q$, an adversarial drift proportional to inventory, structurally identical to GLFT's $q_{\text{eff}}$.

Optimal execution is the dual problem and contributes an endgame pricing rule rather than a schedule. Almgren–Chriss's path is $x(t) = X\sinh(\kappa(T-t))/\sinh(\kappa T)$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}$ ([J. Risk 3(2):5–39](https://doi.org/10.21314/JOR.2001.041)), and with transient impact Obizhaeva–Wang show the optimum is a block at each end plus continuous trading between ([JFM 16(1):1–32](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2012.09.001)). Both are void for us in timing, because our impact has no transient component to wait out. What survives is the marginal-cost rule: carrying one more lot to where it must be dumped costs $\Delta/2 + 2\vartheta q$, so cross whenever expected adverse move plus inventory shadow cost exceeds the half-spread. With a step penalty the shadow cost of the last lots is effectively infinite, so the endgame is to cross rather than quote.

## A queue position is worth about half a spread, and latency barely touches it

The most useful object here is an accounting identity, not a model. By optional stopping on the latent efficient price, $V_t = \alpha_t(\delta_t - AS_t)$, where $\alpha_t = P(\text{fill}\mid\mathcal{F}_t)$, $\delta_t = P_A - P_t$ is the liquidity premium (the half-spread if $P_t$ is the mid) and $AS_t = E[P_{\tau^*} - P_t\mid\mathcal{F}_t,\text{fill}]$ is adverse selection ([Moallemi & Yuan, 2016](https://moallemi.com/ciamac/papers/queue-value-2016.pdf)). Order value is fill probability times premium minus adverse selection, all three functions of queue position. For exponential trade sizes $f(u) = (\theta/2)e^{-\theta|u|}$ with no cancellations, $V(q,\delta) = \alpha(q)\delta - \beta(q)$ with $\alpha(q) = p^+_J + [\mu(1-p^+_J)/(\mu+2\gamma)]e^{-bq}$ and $b \equiv 2\theta = 2/E|u|$, so **the e-folding length of fill probability in queue position is half the mean taker order size**. Fill probability falls with depth ahead, adverse selection rises, and a maximally back-of-queue order is filled only by a price jump.

The empirical magnitudes are large enough to dominate placement and small enough to be forgotten. In large-tick Nasdaq names, order value runs from **0.41 ticks at the front to 0.12 ticks at $8\times10^4$ shares ahead**, fill probability falls 0.78 to 0.605 and adverse selection rises 0.42 to 0.745 ticks, and the cross-section of touch values spans **0.03 to 0.41 ticks** with one negative case; the calibration uses $\delta_0 = 0.8$ ticks, half-spread plus a 0.3-tick rebate ([Moallemi & Yuan, 2016](https://moallemi.com/ciamac/papers/queue-value-2016.pdf)). Half a spread is the right prior, corroborated independently ([Guo, Ruan & Zhu, arXiv:1505.04810](https://arxiv.org/html/1505.04810v2)). Ordering matters as much as level: front-of-queue orders profit, end-of-queue orders "lose money", and depth overshoots the zero-marginal-profit level by $4/3$ under maximum queuing uncertainty ([Yueshen, Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.03371)).

Fill probability is well approximated by three variables. On 232,897 live minimum-size orders posted on Binance BTC-perp over a week (127,051 filled, 54.6% fill rate), the surface runs from about **30% to above 90%** and the regression on normalised near queue, opposite queue and imbalance gives $z = 0.5649 + 0.0159Q^{\text{near}} + 0.1013Q^{\text{opp}} - 0.3166\,\text{imb}$ with **R² = 0.946** ([Albers, Cucuringu, Howison & Shestopaloff, arXiv:2502.18625](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18625v2)). The near-queue coefficient is small and positive, contradicting the stated monotonicity, so the imbalance term is the load-bearing evidence and the sign should not be relied on. Two results are directly implementable: the probability that an order of size $q$ fills completely, conditional on liquidity ahead $LA$, equals that of a minimum-size order with $LA + q$, so size is priced with no new parameters; and an estimate ignoring liquidity *behind* the order is an upper bound on the intermediate-time fill probability.

The famous "<2% fill probability" is a cancellation artefact. Because more than 95% of maker orders are cancelled, historical fill estimates are "implicitly conditioned on the trader's decision *not* to cancel": implied from such data, the fill probability of other participants' orders is "less than 2% ... when the spread is one tick wide and bid and ask queues are balanced, compared to the 50% fill probability we find in the same situation" measured live ([Albers et al., arXiv:2502.18625](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18625v2)); and for a semi-analytic model of the same object with state-dependent queue intensities, see [Lokin & Yu, arXiv:2403.02572](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02572). A factor of 25 separates two estimates of the same quantity, and the difference is entirely the strategy wrapper.

Markout is the right objective and its magnitudes are brutal. Measured 1 second after fill and excluding rebate, markout runs from **−0.058 bp** for front-of-queue orders in a large-near/small-opposite book to **−1.157 bp** for back-of-queue orders in a large-near/large-opposite book, standard deviations 0.8–2.2 bp; the unconditional expected return of those maker orders is about **−0.8 bp, or −0.3 bp net of rebate**, and the naive two-sided strategy loses **−0.43 bp per trade** with a Sharpe of −109 ([arXiv:2502.18625, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18625v2)). The mechanism is a strict trade-off: orders with negative subsequent five-second returns are highly likely to fill, orders with positive returns are not. At the shortest horizons the sign is predetermined, not informative: if the mid immediately after an order's terminal event is unchanged or against the order it must have filled, if favourable it must have been cancelled.

| Near / opposite queue | QP 0–0.1 | 0.1–0.4 | 0.4–0.75 | 0.75–1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large near, small opposite | −0.058 | −0.586 | −0.743 | −0.775 |
| Large near, large opposite | −0.296 | −0.882 | −0.967 | −1.157 |
| Small near, small opposite | −0.562 | −0.711 | −0.622 | −0.677 |
| Small near, large opposite | −0.539 | −0.645 | −0.686 | −0.763 |

Two structural implications follow for a one-tick book. The window between "join the short queue" and "enjoy a positive markout" may not exist: the least-bad cell is front-of-queue in a large near queue facing a small opposite queue, reachable only by posting "when the queue is still small but becomes large shortly thereafter", which usually means posting against the current imbalance. And every queue-value number above is conditional on the spread being pinned, which the Tick Size Pilot confirms is the regime where queueing replaces price competition: widening the tick from $0.01 to $0.05 raised spreads and volatility, lowered price efficiency, and raised market-maker profits by about **40%** while lengthening queues ([McCrary et al.](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~jmccrary/FINAL_SSRN.pdf); [SEC assessment](https://www.sec.gov/files/TICK%20PILOT%20ASSESSMENT%20FINAL%20Aug%202.pdf)). If a future tape has a multi-tick spread, the logic flips to price priority and queue value collapses.

Latency is smaller than the industry narrative suggests in steady state, and large in bursts. If our side's touch receives joins at $\nu_{\text{join}}$ lots per second, realised position is $A_{\text{post}} + \nu_{\text{join}}L$ on average with $L = 5$ ms: about one lot at 200 lots per second, but about 100 lots at 20,000 lots per second, a genuine post-price-change race, which exceeds our entire cap. Our tape averages roughly fifteen events per second, an inter-event gap near 67 ms, so 5 ms usually lands before the next event and the steady-state penalty is negligible; the race case is the only one that matters. The same arithmetic governs cancels: the probability a resting order fills during the 5 ms a cancel is in flight is $1 - e^{-0.005\lambda_{\text{fill}}}$, which is 0.025% at $\lambda_{\text{fill}} = 0.05$ s$^{-1}$ and 0.5% at 1 s$^{-1}$. Only front-of-queue orders face the higher figure, and those are worth holding anyway, which is the precise version of "cancel freely deep in the queue, almost never at the front". Traders already price this: a one-standard-deviation increase in back-of-queue depth lengthens expected order lifetime by about **nine seconds, 35% of the average time to cancellation**, while the same statistic for front-of-queue depth is **6%** ([Dahlström, Financial Review 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/fire.12363)).

## Aggressive trading is a tournament with a five-microsecond entry fee

The latency-arbitrage literature is unambiguous, and its conclusion is that we are the liquidity provider in that trade rather than the sniper. Continuous serial processing means even symmetrically observed public information creates arbitrage rents, because the first message to reach each venue wins ([Budish, Cramton & Shim, QJE 130(4):1547–1621](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv027)). The measurement: **537 races per day for the average FTSE 100 symbol**; the modal winner beats the first loser by **5–10 microseconds**; races are about **20% of FTSE 350 volume**; the top **six firms** take over **80%** of wins and losses; the average race is worth about half a price tick; and the aggregate is a **0.42 bp tax** on volume, roughly $4.8bn globally in 2018 and $7bn in 2020, or **33% of the effective spread** ([Aquilina, Budish & O'Neill, QJE 137(1):493–564](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjab032); [BIS WP 955](https://www.bis.org/publ/work955.pdf)). Budish et al.'s own quantified example is a single instrument pair worth about $75m per year. Physics sets the floor: the fastest commercial microwave path from CME Aurora to Equinix NY4 is **3.96 ms one-way** ([Bhattacherjee et al., ACM IMC 2020](https://users.cs.duke.edu/~bmm/assets/pubs/BhattacherjeeALMS20.pdf)), so at 5 ms we are roughly 500 times slower than the modal winning margin and one inter-venue hop behind. No published estimate of a latency tax exists for a single venue, so even the defensive version of this literature must be imported by analogy.

The best evidence that speed is not edge comes from a regulator. On UK message-level data the FCA finds "no evidence that HFTs systematically anticipate near-simultaneous marketable orders sent to different trading venues", but "when moving from very short periods (milliseconds) to longer durations (seconds or tens of seconds), we find patterns consistent with HFTs anticipating the order flow" ([FCA Occasional Paper 16](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-16.pdf)). That endorses a seconds-scale budget and refutes the millisecond one. Whether order anticipation is profitable is unpublished; the FCA explicitly declines to conclude on detriment.

The sign of aggressive versus passive profitability is resolved and favours aggression *when there is a signal*. HFTs "facilitate price efficiency by trading in the direction of permanent price changes and in the opposite direction of transitory pricing errors ... This is done through their liquidity demanding orders. In contrast, HFTs' liquidity supplying orders are adversely selected. The direction of HFTs' trading predicts price changes over short horizons measured in seconds" ([Brogaard, Hendershott & Riordan, RFS 27(8):2267–2306](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/27/8/2267/1582754)). Hit rates stay near a coin flip even for the best firms: Virtu's CEO put about **51–52% of trades profitable** ([NBER WP 29011](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29011/w29011.pdf)), so the money is in sizing winners and avoiding adverse fills.

The cleanest PnL decomposition is one firm, and it maps onto our scoring function. Menkveld's HFT market maker earned a net **€1.55 on the spread but lost €0.68 on its position** per trade, 80% passive, and the position loss decomposes into **+€0.45 for positions held under five seconds and −€1.13 for longer ones** ([Menkveld, JFM 16(4):712–740](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2013.06.006); [Tinbergen 11-076](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/11076.pdf)). Our score, cash plus markout on leftover inventory minus penalty, is the same two-term decomposition. That five-second split is the sharpest calibration in the survey for a markout-scored setting: positions held for seconds carry positive expected alpha, positions held for minutes do not. Firm dispersion is enormous, with median annualised Sharpe **4.3** and four-factor alpha 22.02% in E-mini, a top decile earning **$2.01 per contract against $0.46 for the median**, and passive-HFT Sharpe 5.85 versus aggressive 4.29 in one draft ([Baron, Brogaard, Hagströmer & Kirilenko, JFQA 54(3):993–1024](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109018001096)). Style-level numbers are unstable across drafts and all are gross of the cost of speed.

Cascades are the one aggressive family whose timescale fits comfortably. On 6 May 2010 a single program sold 75,000 E-mini contracts, liquidity fell to "less than 1% of its depth from that morning's level", HFTs flipped from absorbing to selling at 13:42, and prices returned to the 13:32 level by 14:08, a **36-minute round trip** ([CFTC–SEC Staff Report](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/%40otherif/documents/ifdocs/staff-findings050610.pdf)). The peer-reviewed verdict is careful: HFTs "did not trigger the Flash Crash, but their responses ... exacerbated market volatility" ([Kirilenko, Kyle, Samadi & Tuzun, J. Finance 72(3):967–998](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12498)). The transferable statistic is the hot-potato ratio, $|\Delta\,\text{net taker position}|/\text{taker volume}$ on a five-second moving average: near zero with high volume means intermediaries are passing risk rather than absorbing it. The FCA's ex-post finding is the encouraging one for a fast-but-not-fastest participant: during flash events HFTs "on average make small profits ... unlike hybrid firms that make a loss", attributed to "better manage their risks and avoid adverse selection" ([FCA research note, 2018](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research/research-note-participants-behave-flash-events-evidence.pdf)). The profit came from risk management, not from picking the bottom.

| Strategy family | Bucket | Required speed | Reported magnitude | Transfers here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Latency arbitrage, sniping | Out of reach | Winner beats loser by 5–10 µs | 0.42 bp tax; ~half a tick per race | No: single venue, no second quote to race |
| Index / cross-venue arbitrage | Out of reach | Sub-millisecond; 3.96 ms physics floor | ~$75m/yr for one pair | No: one instrument, one venue |
| Top-of-book queue rent | Out of reach | Colocation; revenue ranked by latency | +€1.55 spread, −€0.68 position per trade | Partly: the mechanism, not the ranking |
| Order anticipation at ms | Absent empirically | Milliseconds | None published | No |
| Order anticipation at seconds | Usable | Seconds to tens of seconds | Not quantified | Yes, as flow statistics |
| Aggressive taking on a seconds signal | Usable | Seconds | Demanding orders informed; supplying adversely selected | Yes, as a take gate |
| Cascade participation | Usable | Onset in ~1 s; event lasts minutes | HFTs profit, hybrids lose | Only if the tape produces cascades |
| Spoofing, layering, ignition, pinging | Void | Milliseconds; needs deception | JPM $920m penalty | No: the tape never reacts to us |

Fees decide the aggressive/passive trade-off in real markets and are absent here. US maker-taker exchanges charge about 30 cents per hundred shares to take and pay about 29 to make, inverted venues price the opposite way, and the 2024 reform cut the access-fee cap from 30 to 10 mils ([Reg NMS final rule, 89 FR](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-10-08/pdf/2024-21867.pdf)). In our units 29 cents per share is about 0.058 bp on a $50 stock, an order of magnitude below a typical quoted spread, so rebates modify a strategy but cannot rescue negative spread capture. The tick-size results do transfer, because they concern the discreteness of the grid rather than fees.

## Deep learning predicts labels; validation is the actual deliverable

Three results settle the ML question, none about architecture. Architecture is not the binding constraint: on the same task, features and dataset, "the Multilayer Perceptron performs comparably to or better than state-of-the-art CNN-LSTM architectures", implying the spatial and temporal biases are a decent approximation of book dynamics but not the true dimensions ([Briola, Turiel & Aste, arXiv:2007.07319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07319)). Generalisation failure is temporal: testing 15 state-of-the-art models in a fixed harness, "all models exhibit a significant performance drop when exposed to new data" ([Prata et al., AI Review 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). And the one genuinely deep result carries no PnL: a model trained on about 500 Nasdaq stocks over 17 months, with hundreds of millions of observations and 500 GPU nodes, beats stock-specific linear VAR-probit models by **5–10 percentage points of accuracy**, while noting that "an increase of even 1% in accuracy is considered a significant improvement" and that whether this is profitable "requires a careful analysis of transaction costs ... beyond the scope of this paper" ([Sirignano & Cont, Quantitative Finance 19(9):1449–1459](http://rama.cont.perso.math.cnrs.fr/pdf/SirignanoCont2019.pdf)). The mechanism behind the universality is a queueing relation between depth imbalance and the next move, which is the hand-crafted feature set; the net's contribution is evidence that the map is stable across assets.

The compute and data scale of credible work is three to five orders of magnitude beyond a one-file, one-tape project: the tick-label benchmark alone ran **135 experiments and 959 GPU-hours**, and Sirignano–Cont used 500 GPU nodes. There is no published deep-LOB result at "one instrument, one day, laptop". Reinforcement learning is worse, because where an analytic policy exists RL rediscovers it: credible results either approximate the optimum of a model they specify as a numerical method for a high-dimensional PDE ([Guéant & Manziuk, Applied Mathematical Finance 26(5)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13205)), or beat a fixed-distance baseline rather than a hard-constrained GLFT policy. Even the best market-making RL study reports a consolidated agent at **15.43 ± 13.01** normalised daily PnL against **2.80 ± 10.30** for a fixed symmetric strategy, with mean absolute position falling from 2678 to 104 and negative mean PnL on two of ten securities ([Spooner et al., AAMAS 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.04216)). Its transferable content is the reward, not the network: asymmetric dampening of the directional component, with $\eta \approx 0.1$ the point where the agent converges to near-zero inventory, is the risk aversion the analytic models impose through $\gamma$.

Validation is where this literature earns its keep, and it supplies four defences that published LOB papers use at most one of. Time-ordered splits with **purging and embargo** remove overlap between a label window and a test observation, with a default embargo of 1% of the sample after each test fold, and combinatorial purged cross-validation yields a distribution of backtests instead of one path ([López de Prado, *Advances in Financial Machine Learning*, Wiley 2018, ch. 7](https://www.quantresearch.org/Innovations.htm)). The **probability of backtest overfitting** formalises selection: split the performance matrix of $N$ configurations by $T$ observations, pick the in-sample best in each combination, record its out-of-sample rank, and PBO is the probability that rank falls below the median, which sits far above 0.5 for realistic trial counts even on data with no signal ([Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu, J. Computational Finance 20(4)](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-prob.pdf)). The expected maximum in-sample Sharpe grows like $\sqrt{2\ln N/T}$, which is the minimum-backtest-length argument ([Bailey et al., Notices of the AMS 61(5)](https://carmamaths.org/jon/backtest.pdf)), and the **deflated Sharpe ratio** corrects for it with a stated threshold of **DSR > 0.95** ([Bailey & López de Prado, JPM 40(5)](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/deflated-sharpe.pdf)). For comparison, the best-case base rate in the adjacent literature is **82.4%** factor replication once a Bayesian hierarchical model replaces naive t-stats ([Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, J. Finance 78(5):2465–2518](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13249)); intraday strategies have no equivalent study.

Applied at our scale the arithmetic is unforgiving. Local measurement puts the standard error of a round-2 PnL of about 500k at roughly **26k**, on a single 6,000-second realisation dominated by a handful of jumps. Anything under about 50k of improvement is not evidence, and a 13k difference between variants is noise. That is minimum-backtest-length applied to one tape, and it is the strongest argument in this report for treating the per-round search intensity, dozens of variants, as the quantity to correct for rather than the source of discoveries.

Three experiments the evidence says not to run, and which will keep being proposed: a deep LOB model (compute and label problems are structural and the decision-level metric is below the spread), VPIN or any volume-bucketed toxicity meter (it fails after controlling for volume and volatility), and a Hawkes-branching-ratio alpha (on a Poisson tape it measures the generator, and equating it with criticality imports a law from a market that has self-excitation).

The honest gaps are specific. There is no verified measurement of OFI R² at sub-second or event-level horizons; the literature jumps from event-level mechanics to 10-second aggregates. There is no verified numeric comparison of microprice against weighted mid against mid, because the abstract is qualitative and the body paywalled. There is no published day-to-day stability study for queue-reactive intensity curves, and no queue-aware placement optimiser on a discrete tick grid, which is the central theoretical gap for our regime. No paper solves market making with an indicator-type running cost, a fixed dollar amount per second outside a band. No source measures the cost of a 5 ms delay, how much queue-imbalance edge survives it, or the half-life of LOB feature-to-return relationships. No RL result reports net-of-fees, net-of-latency PnL on real data with a live record, and Moallemi and Sağlam's quantitative latency cost could not be extracted. And there is essentially no literature on single-venue, zero-fee, integer-tick markets, which is ours: every magnitude above comes from a fragmented, fee-bearing, multi-venue setting and should be read as an upper bound on what is contested, not a forecast of what is available. Nine candidate arXiv identifiers that surfaced during the underlying research resolved to unrelated papers and were replaced; this report cites only identifiers re-verified against arXiv, DOI or publisher metadata, and where a quantity is asserted without a number the source itself is qualitative.

## Conclusion

The useful split in this literature is not between good and bad papers but between three kinds of claim, which fail differently here. Accounting identities, such as $V = \alpha(\delta - AS)$ and $\Delta P = \beta\,\mathrm{OFI}$, must be implemented because they are exact given the state. Optimal-control results survive only as architectures and constraints, because every closed form assumes a continuous grid, Poisson fills at a continuous distance and no queue, and the tick grid censors the one term practitioners most want to import, the spread floor. Empirical magnitudes mostly do not survive, because they are measured where the participant's own orders move the price and where most visible liquidity is withdrawn within seconds; a fixed tape removes both that cost and an entire class of strategies.

What remains is a narrower and more defensible programme than the literature's surface suggests. The tradable object is not a price forecast but a joint distribution of fill and markout conditioned on queue state, which is exactly what our simulator can measure and no real dataset can measure cleanly, because in real data the counterfactual queue position is unobservable. The 0.06–0.15 round-trip completion probability, the −0.37 out-of-sample OFI R² and the −109 Sharpe of a naive two-sided maker are not reasons for pessimism; they mark where the remaining edge cannot be. It has to sit in the interaction between inventory pressure and queue position, at a horizon of seconds, with a hard constraint enforced structurally rather than priced.
